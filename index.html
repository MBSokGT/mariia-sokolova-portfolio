Branching Dialogue Scenario
ODK — Teaching Product Knowledge Through a Real Sales Call
+
iSpring SuiteDialogue simulation75 completions~3 min read
The Challenge

Most reps already knew the product facts. What they needed practice with was handling client objections and calculating cost on the spot.

Design Decisions

Built as a scripted conversation between the company's travelling manager and a bar owner. Cost math appears inside the conversation, at the point a rep would actually need it — not as a separate reference table.

Outcome

100% of respondents rated it clear or very clear. The most useful feedback: the simulated client agrees too easily — a "difficult client" branch is the clear next iteration.

Interactive Storybook Course
C.O.D.E. — Turning a Crowded Category Into a Story
+
iSpring SuiteStorybook / pirate format188 completions~4 min read
The Challenge

C.O.D.E. is a cordial line entering a market with well-known, trusted competitors. Reps needed more than facts — they needed to credibly explain why it's worth choosing over what bartenders already trust.

Design Decisions

Built as a page-turning storybook tied to the product's own maritime branding, opening with the real history of cordials before the pitch. A direct cordial-vs-syrup comparison pre-empts the question reps get constantly from clients. The quiz includes open-ended scenarios, not just recall.

Outcome

145 of 188 reps rated it 9–10 (NPS 70.2); 94.7% found it clear. A vocal minority found the pirate framing too playful for a workplace training, and one free-text quiz answer format was too strict — both are now things I check for before launch.

Interactive Longread
Critical & Creative Thinking — Building a Course From Scratch Mid-Crisis
+
iSpring SuiteCustom SVG illustrationsAdapted from source material~3 min read
The Challenge

The company lost access to its ready-made course library — a budget cut, not a planned change — right as individual development plans (IDPs) became the department's top priority. Soft-skills training suddenly had to exist to support that, with no library left to pull from.

Design Decisions

Adapted an English-language source instead of writing from zero, but cut its redundancy rather than translating it straight. Built custom SVG illustrations to replace the visual assets lost with the library, plus full cover and banner assets so it read as finished, not an emergency patch.

Outcome

Managers came away with practical tools they could apply to their day-to-day work, not just abstract theory — which was the point of tying it to the IDP process in the first place.

Longread
"Фактор Вкуса" (Flavor Factor) — Turning a Factory Tour Into a Sales Argument
+
iSpring Suite7 sectionsSME-reviewed via tracked changes~3 min read
The Challenge

Sales managers were selling a product without a credible answer to "how is this actually made?" — the kind of question that builds trust with a client deciding between suppliers.

Design Decisions

Structured around the stages of production across seven sections, so reps could read start to finish or jump to whatever a client just asked about. Edited with tracked changes so production-side experts could review and approve technical content without losing a record of what changed. Paired with an internal announcement so the release didn't launch silently.

Outcome

It landed well beyond its intended audience — people outside sales read it out of genuine interest in how the facility works. For reps, production became something they could explain, including concrete lead times clients ask about when ordering specific flavors.

Internal Communications
Company Digest — Making the Whole Organization Visible to Itself
+
Writer / EditorCross-department~3 min read
The Challenge

In a company where teams rarely see each other's work, there wasn't a regular, readable way for people across departments to see commercial results, market context, and what other teams were actually doing.

Design Decisions

Paired commercial results with outside context — restaurant openings, HoReCa trends — so numbers meant something. Every draft got a pass to cut generic, AI-sounding phrasing, so it read like a person wrote it for colleagues. Over time, managers started using it to share personal wins too, not just work updates — one issue covered an employee who ran a marathon and won an award, sitting right alongside commercial results.

Outcome

People across departments got a clearer picture of how their work connects to the company's results. After one issue, the CEO personally praised the employees mentioned in it — a sign it was being read at the top of the company, not just circulated.
